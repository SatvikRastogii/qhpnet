\begin{abstract}
A wireless sensor network's lifetime is decided by three choices: which nodes may become cluster heads, which of them serve and who joins whom, and how heads reach the sink. We propose QHP-Net, which gives each choice its own bio-inspired mechanism, each aimed at a failure mode measured in our earlier nine-protocol study. Bacterial quorum sensing with a refractory term picks rested, locally strong candidates. Hippopotamus Optimisation selects heads by minimising the predicted bottleneck stress $\max_i \text{drain}_i/E_i$, and members join whichever head, or the sink directly, minimises their stress contribution. An ant-pheromone link memory learns per-link delivery probability from ARQ acknowledgements alone and drives ETX-aware inter-cluster forwarding. We evaluated QHP-Net against eight protocols, including NSGA-II, the best full-yield protocol of the earlier study, and our earlier HIECF framework. All protocols run in one engine with energy conservation asserted, over 30 paired topologies on lossy and ideal channels. On the lossy channel QHP-Net's first node dies at round 2,133, which is 249 rounds (13.2\%) after NSGA-II's and 43--52\% after the fuzzy, DQN and HIECF selectors. It wins in all 30 topologies (paired $t(29)=46.2$, $p_{Holm}<10^{-27}$, $d_z=8.4$, confirmed by Wilcoxon). It delivers 98.3\% of readings at 16\% less energy per reading, and reaches \fndfrac\% of a physical upper bound on first-node death. Unlike the learned selectors of the earlier study, its gain persists when packet loss is removed (+168 rounds). An ablation shows that each selection and clustering mechanism contributes 42--328 rounds. It also shows that routing on learned link quality is as good as routing on the true link quality, and that a Physarum congestion router performs worse.
\end{abstract}
